Native Hawaiian women experience disproportionately high rates of metabolic and liver disease compared to White women, but the molecular mechanisms behind this disparity are not well understood. This study conducted a genome-wide epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) using the Illumina EPIC array (802,978 CpG sites) in blood samples from 324 postmenopausal women, comparing DNA methylation patterns between Native Hawaiian and White participants.

We identified 736 differentially methylated loci (DMLs) between the two groups. Pathway analysis linked 61% of the genes associated with these DMLs to liver disease pathways, suggesting an epigenetic contribution to the elevated liver disease risk observed in Native Hawaiian populations. We further examined how these methylation differences related to dietary patterns, finding associations with adherence to the DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) diet, as well as with circulating levels of adiponectin, a hormone involved in regulating glucose and fatty acid metabolism.

These findings suggest that population-level differences in DNA methylation may help explain disparities in metabolic and liver disease risk, and point to diet-related and hormonal pathways as potential mechanisms linking epigenetic variation to health outcomes in this understudied population.

*I was co-first author on this paper, contributing to the statistical analysis of the EWAS data and the downstream pathway and clinical correlation analyses.*
